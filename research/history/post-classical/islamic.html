The Islamic period brought Aramean communities under new political systems that extended from the early caliphates to the Ottoman advance into the Levant. The change involved conquest, new fiscal and legal arrangements, shifting relationships between Muslim rulers and Christian or Jewish institutions, and gradual changes in language and religious affiliation. These processes did not occur at the same speed or with the same result in Syria, Upper Mesopotamia, Mesopotamia, Anatolia, or the Iranian borderlands. Aramean communities adapted through churches, monasteries, schools, urban professions, rural networks, and changing forms of bilingual and multilingual life.

Part I: Conquest and Political Realignment

AD 637–661

The End of Byzantine and Sasanian Control

The Arab-Muslim conquests followed prolonged wars between the Byzantine and Sasanian empires. The battles of Yarmouk in 636 AD and al-Qadisiyyah in 636 AD weakened the existing military and fiscal systems in Syria and Iraq. By 637 AD, Arab-Muslim forces had taken or secured major centers including Damascus and the Ctesiphon region, although the incorporation of districts continued through campaigns, agreements, and local negotiations.

Aramean communities did not respond as one political group. Some cities and local rulers negotiated terms, while other populations experienced military violence, displacement, taxation, or changes in landholding. Religious divisions within the Byzantine Christian world could influence local attitudes, but it is not possible to infer a single Aramean reaction from later accounts of the conquest.

The first caliphal administrations relied on existing cities, agricultural systems, officials, and local elites. This produced both continuity and change: older institutions could survive under new authority, while taxation, military control, and political allegiance were reorganized.

Part II: Early Caliphal Administration and Christian Communities

AD 661–750

Fiscal Obligations and Local Institutions

Under the Umayyad caliphs, administration expanded across Syria, Mesopotamia, and adjacent regions. Christian and Jewish communities were increasingly governed through categories that later Islamic legal writing associated with protected non-Muslim subjects. In practice, the position of a community depended on the ruler, the district, existing agreements, fiscal policy, and local power relationships.

Taxes on persons, land, and agricultural production were important to the caliphal state, but the terms and collection methods changed over time. Bishops, patriarchs, village leaders, and urban notables could mediate between communities and officials, while local courts and religious institutions continued to handle many internal matters. Such autonomy existed within political subordination and could be limited by officials or changing policy.

Later texts about agreements attributed to Umar preserve important evidence about legal ideals and communal memory, but they should not be treated as a single, unchanged contract governing every Christian community from the beginning of Islamic rule. Restrictions, protections, and enforcement varied by place and period.

Part III: Abbasid Rule, Language Change, and Aramean Adaptation

AD 750–945

Arabic Administration and Diverse Local Outcomes

The Abbasid revolution moved the political center toward Iraq and made Baghdad a major imperial capital. Arabic became increasingly important in government, legal documentation, public culture, and urban advancement, especially after administrative reforms associated with Abd al-Malik and later caliphal governments. The shift did not immediately eliminate Aramaic. Syriac, Jewish Babylonian Aramaic, Mandaic, and other languages continued in particular religious, scholarly, and local settings.

The movement from Aramaic to Arabic as a daily language was gradual and regionally uneven. Urban administration, intermarriage, schooling, military service, taxation, trade, and religious affiliation could all influence language choice. Some communities became Arabic-speaking while retaining Christian institutions; others maintained Aramaic dialects alongside Arabic for much longer. Language change should not be treated as a single empire-wide event or as proof of the disappearance of Aramean identity.

Conversion to Islam also occurred over long periods and for different reasons. Fiscal obligations, social mobility, marriage, patronage, conviction, and local political conditions could all play a role, while many Christian and Jewish communities remained in place. The surviving evidence does not support one uniform demographic model for all Aramean regions.

Part IV: Schools, Translation, and Aramean Christian Scholarship

AD 945–1258

Syriac and Arabic Intellectual Networks

Christian scholars from Syriac-speaking communities participated in the transmission of Greek medical, philosophical, and scientific texts into Arabic. Hunayn ibn Ishaq and other translators worked within networks that included Christian, Muslim, Jewish, and court-sponsored scholars. Their work often involved Greek, Syriac, and Arabic, but not every translation followed one fixed two-stage process.

Baghdad, Mosul, Harran, Nisibis, and other centers supported different forms of scholarship, medicine, theology, and manuscript production. The House of Wisdom is associated with Abbasid learning, but its exact organization and role should not be expanded into a single institution responsible for all translation activity. Aramean Christian scholars contributed to these networks as members of particular families, churches, schools, and professional circles.

Monasteries and scriptoria also continued copying Syriac texts, developing liturgical traditions, and preserving local histories. This was not an absolute insulation of Aramaic from Arabic: many scholars and communities were multilingual, and Syriac writing changed through contact with Arabic-speaking environments.

Part V: Mongol Rule and the Reorganization of Aramean Communities

AD 1258–1401

Baghdad, the Ilkhanate, and Regional Disruption

The Mongol capture of Baghdad in 1258 AD ended Abbasid political authority in the city and disrupted established systems of taxation, landholding, scholarship, and trade. Aramean Christian communities experienced the conquest differently according to location and ecclesiastical affiliation. Some leaders gained access to Mongol courts or protected positions, while other communities suffered violence, displacement, loss of property, or the collapse of urban institutions.

The Ilkhanate did not follow one constant religious policy. Mongol rulers and their officials included people of different religious backgrounds, and periods of favor toward Christian institutions were followed by policies that strengthened Islam or responded to political conflict. The presence of Christian figures at court did not guarantee safety for all Aramean Christians.

Bar Hebraeus and other Syriac writers recorded the effects of political change while participating in scholarly and ecclesiastical networks. Their writings are valuable historical evidence, but they represent particular communities and viewpoints rather than the experience of every Aramean population.

Part VI: Timurid Destruction and the Ottoman Transition

AD 1401–1516

Late Medieval Political Fragmentation

Timur's campaigns after 1401 AD damaged cities, agricultural districts, and Christian institutions across parts of Mesopotamia and the surrounding regions. The effects were severe in some urban centers, but the scale and duration of disruption differed by locality. Aramean communities did not disappear as a single population; they reorganized around villages, monasteries, mountain regions, urban neighborhoods, and ecclesiastical networks.

Christian patriarchates and local churches continued to govern religious life, education, charity, and community identity, although their territory and political influence changed. Arabic was dominant in many public and daily contexts, while literary Syriac and regional Aramaic varieties remained important in worship, scholarship, and communal memory. These linguistic roles were not identical in every church or settlement.

The Ottoman conquest of much of Syria in 1516 AD began a new political framework. It did not mark the end of Aramean communities or Syriac traditions, but it changed the imperial institutions through which they negotiated taxation, property, leadership, and security.
