The campaigns of Alexander the Great and the subsequent establishment of the Seleucid Empire (312–64 BC) fundamentally transformed the administrative and political landscape of Syria and Upper Mesopotamia. For the native Aramean population, the Hellenistic era introduced a dual dynamic: while Koine Greek became the elite language of civic government and military garrisons, Aramaic remained the undisputed spoken vernacular. Across three centuries, Aramean society adapted through bilingualism, local semi-autonomous dynasties, and the fragmentation of Imperial Aramaic into distinct regional scripts.

Part I: The Macedonian Conquest & Seleucid Foundation

333–301 BC

The Fall of Achaemenid Rule and the Rise of Seleucus I

Alexander the Great’s victory at Issus in 333 BC shattered Persian rule across Syria and the Levant. Following Alexander’s death in 323 BC, his generals (the Diadochi) engaged in two decades of warfare to partition his empire. By 312 BC, Seleucus I Nicator secured Babylonia, Upper Mesopotamia, and Syria, formally founding the Seleucid Empire.

To anchor Macedonian rule, Seleucus established the Syrian Tetrapolis—an urban network comprising Antioch on the Orontes, Seleucia in Pieria, Apamea, and Laodicea. These Greek poleis introduced Greek civic councils and gymnasia, creating a Greek-speaking ruling class above the indigenous Aramean agrarian population.

Part II: Ethnic Terminology & Garrison Settlements

c. 300–200 BC

Fortress Settlements and Ethnic Redefinition

During the early 3rd century BC, Seleucid kings constructed heavily fortified garrisons along trade arteries. A prominent example was Dura-Europos, founded around 300 BC on the Euphrates. Originally named Dura (the Aramaic word for "fortress"), the site combined Macedonian grid architecture with a mixed population of Aramean merchants and Greek troops.

This era also cemented the Greek term "Syrian" as the official administrative designation for the native Aramean populations of the Levant, merging older regional Semitic identities under a single provincial framework.

Part III: Language Shift & Script Diversification

c. 250–150 BC

The Branching of Aramaic Script Traditions

As Koine Greek replaced Imperial Aramaic as the official chancery language for state decrees and coinage, the standardized Aramaic alphabet of the Persian era began to break apart. Without a central Achaemenid chancery, local Aramaic-speaking populations developed distinct written scripts:

  • Syriac (Edessan Aramaic): Emerged in Upper Mesopotamia as a refined literary cursive.
  • Palmyrene Aramaic: Formed in the oasis of Tadmor for trade agreements and monumental tombs.
  • Nabataean Aramaic: Adopted across Transjordan and Northern Arabia for legal inscriptions.

See also: Ancient Language for the evolution and epigraphy of these regional scripts.

Part IV: Seleucid Decline & Rise of Local Kingdoms

c. 150–83 BC

Emergence of Independent Aramean & Arab Principates

Dynastic civil wars and military defeats against the Parthian Empire severely weakened central Seleucid authority by the mid-2nd century BC. Native local dynasties seized the opportunity to form independent or semi-autonomous states across Syria and Upper Mesopotamia:

  • Kingdom of Osroene (132 BC): Centered at Edessa (Urhay), where an Aramean-Arab dynasty made Syriac the court language.
  • Palmyrene Merchant Republic: Expanded control over caravan trade routes traversing the Syrian Desert.
  • Kingdom of Commagene: Established in south-central Anatolia, featuring syncretic Greco-Aramean royal sanctuaries.

Part V: Syncretic Cults & Religious Life

300–64 BC

Interpretatio Graeca and the Syrian Goddess

Religiously, the Hellenistic period was characterized by deep syncretism. Native West Semitic deities were systematically associated with Greek Olympian gods, allowing Arameans to maintain their ancestral cults under Greek names (interpretatio graeca):

  • Hadad of Hierapolis: Equated with Zeus and worshipped alongside Atargatis (Dea Syria, "The Syrian Goddess") at Bambyce (Manbij). Her temple complex attracted international pilgrims and was later immortalized in Lucian of Samosata’s famous treatise.
  • Bel & Baalshamin: Venerated across Syrian oases as supreme sky and weather deities, merging Semitic solar concepts with Hellenistic iconography.

Part VI: Armenian Interregnum & Roman Annexation

83–64 BC

Pompey the Great and the End of Hellenistic Rule

By 83 BC, internal strife reduced the Seleucid state to a weak rump power in western Syria, leading the citizens of Antioch to temporarily invite Tigranes the Great of Armenia to take control.

Following Rome's victory in the Third Mithridatic War, Roman general Pompey the Great entered Syria in 64 BC. Pompey deposed the last Seleucid monarch, Antiochus XIII, and annexed the region as the Roman province of Syria, ending 250 years of Hellenistic political rule while preserving its Greco-Aramaic culture.
