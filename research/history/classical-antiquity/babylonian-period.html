Period: 610-539 BC


As the Assyrian Empire crumbled, the Neo-Babylonian Empire rose to fill the power vacuum. Under Nebuchadnezzar II (605-562 BC), Babylon achieved unprecedented magnificence. For Arameans, this period represented a shift from harsh Assyrian militarism to more pragmatic, trade-focused Babylonian governance. Though Aramean political independence in the west remained impossible, the Neo-Babylonian period allowed greater cultural and economic integration, particularly for the vast Aramean populations thriving in Babylonia itself.

Part I: Levantine Conquest & Integration (610-582 BC)

The Western Consolidation

Following the collapse of Assyria, Nabopolassar (625-605 BC) and his son Nebuchadnezzar II systematically took over former Assyrian territories. Aramean regions in the west fell under Babylonian hegemony through a combination of military conquest and strategic submissions:

  • 608 BC - Carchemish campaign; Aramean territories in the middle Euphrates valley submit to Babylon.
  • 605 BC - Nebuchadnezzar II defeats the Egyptians at Carchemish and consolidates control over Syria.
  • 597 BC - Fall of Jerusalem; establishment of firm Babylonian overlordship in the southern Levant.
  • 590-582 BC - Final military sweeps against remaining western resistance; total provincial dominance is established.

Unlike the highly militarized Assyrian provincial model, Babylonian rule focused primarily on direct economic extraction. Local West Semitic elites who cooperated were often left in place to manage regional affairs and collect imperial tributes.

Part II: Deportations & Imperial Administration

Population Transfers and the Scribal Class

Contrary to common belief, the Neo-Babylonian Empire practiced mass deportations. However, rather than utilizing the bidirectional transfers of Assyria, the Babylonians brought massive numbers of captured Arameans, Phoenicians, and Judeans directly to the Babylonian heartland to work on imperial construction projects and cultivate state lands.

At the administrative level, Official Aramaic remained the undisputed bureaucratic language. Trained Aramean scribes, already highly integrated under the late Assyrian administration, continued to run the day-to-day imperial machinery:

  • Bureaucracy - Aramean scribes managed taxation records, civil correspondence, and administrative logistics.
  • Military & Trade - Aramean merchants managed extensive riverine and overland commerce, while Aramean recruits served in Babylonian defensive garrisons.

Part III: Arameans and Chaldeans in Southern Babylonia

Tribal Dynamics of the South

During this period, southern Mesopotamia (Babylonia) was characterized by a complex mix of local Babylonians, Chaldeans, and Arameans. While closely allied, the Arameans (like the Gambulu, Puqudu, and Ru'ua) and Chaldeans (like the Bit-Yakini and Bit-Dakkuri) were distinct tribal groups.

Under the Chaldean-descended kings of Babylon, these southern tribal populations enjoyed immense wealth and political power. Extensive intermarriage between tribal nobility and the Babylonian elite created a highly integrated, hybrid culture, cementing southern Mesopotamia as a powerhouse of West Semitic and Aramaic culture.

Part IV: Trade, Economy & Syncretic Religion

The Pax Babylonica

Because the Neo-Babylonians prioritized trade over endless military expansion, Aramean merchants thrived. They commanded massive trade networks, operating overland caravan routes between the Persian Gulf, the Levant, and Egypt.

In terms of religion, Aramean communities successfully maintained traditional worship. Cults of Aramean deities like Hadad, Attar, and Shamash flourished, frequently blending with native Babylonian cults. In urban centers, Hadad was often equated with Marduk, allowing Arameans to fully participate in Babylonian civic life without abandoning their religious identity.

Part V: Nabonidus & the Harran-Tayma Connection (556-539 BC)

The Aramean Influence at Court

The culmination of Aramean cultural influence occurred during the reign of Nabonidus (556-539 BC). His mother, Adad-guppi, was a high priestess of the moon god Sîn at the northern Aramean religious center of Harran. Nabonidus's deep religious devotion to Sîn, coupled with his rebuilding of the Ehulhul temple in Harran, caused a severe rift with the traditional Marduk priesthood of Babylon.

Furthermore, Nabonidus spent ten years of his reign at the north Arabian oasis of Tayma, a major Aramaic-speaking trade hub, leaving his son Belshazzar to rule as regent in Babylon. This period marked the peak of West Semitic cultural and political influence within the Mesopotamian imperial structure.

Part VI: Fall of Babylon & Transition to Persian Rule (539 BC)

Linguistic and Cultural Continuity

Internal political division enabled Cyrus II (the Great) to conquer Babylon in 539 BC, absorbing it into the Achaemenid Persian Empire. For the vast Aramean population, this political transition was remarkably smooth. Cyrus maintained local administrative systems, and because Aramaic was already the undisputed lingua franca of administrative communication across the Near East, the Persians adopted it as the official language of their vast empire.
