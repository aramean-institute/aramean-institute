The evidence first records Aramean groups in the Late Bronze and early Iron Ages. The disruptions that followed the Late Bronze Age created new conditions for mobile, semi-mobile, and settled communities across northern Mesopotamia and the Levant. Over the following centuries, these communities developed different political centers and regional relationships rather than forming a single state.


During the Iron Age, Aramean kingdoms emerged across the Levant and Upper Mesopotamia. States such as Bit-Adini, Bit-Agusi, Hamath, Damascus, and Sam'al participated in regional trade, warfare, and shifting alliances. From the ninth through the seventh centuries BC, Assyrian expansion progressively brought many of these kingdoms and communities into an imperial system through tribute, conquest, annexation, deportation, and provincial administration.


After the fall of the Neo-Assyrian Empire, Aramean communities lived under Babylonian and Persian rule before encountering the Macedonian and Hellenistic kingdoms established after Alexander's conquests. Roman and Parthian power later competed across Syria, Mesopotamia, and the regions east of the Euphrates. In late antiquity, Byzantine and Sasanian rivalry further changed the political boundaries in which Aramean communities lived, while Syriac Christian institutions became important centers of community organization and historical memory.


From the seventh century AD, the Islamic conquests brought Aramean communities under successive caliphates and regional dynasties. Political authority, taxation, legal arrangements, and relations between communities changed over time and differed across Syria, Upper Mesopotamia, Mesopotamia, Anatolia, and the Iranian borderlands. Ottoman rule later incorporated much of these regions into a changing imperial system shaped by provincial administration, reform, war, centralization, and the upheavals of the First World War.


Since 1918, the collapse of the Ottoman order and the creation of new states and borders have shaped modern Aramean history. Mass violence and displacement, including the Sayfo, separated many communities from their towns and villages and produced new patterns of migration. Across the Middle East and the diaspora, Aramean communities have continued to rebuild institutions and negotiate political, social, and cultural change.
