330 - 637 CE

The Byzantine Era (330-637 CE) represented a highly complex and often adversarial chapter for the Aramean populations of the Levant and Mesopotamia. Following the Christianization of the Roman Empire, local Aramean identity became deeply intertwined with ecclesiastical politics. The theological controversies of the 5th century - most notably the Council of Chalcedon - severely fractured relations between the imperial court in Constantinople and the West Syrian (Aramaic-speaking) population. This era was characterized by periods of intense state-sponsored religious persecution, offset by strategic periods of political relief, clandestine survival, and institutional migration.

Part I: The Council of Chalcedon and Ecclesiastical Rupture

The Christological Divide

In 451 CE, Emperor Marcian convened the Council of Chalcedon to resolve ongoing disputes regarding the nature of Christ. The council promulgated the dyophysite formula, asserting that Christ coexisted "in two natures" (divine and human) after the Incarnation. To the majority of Aramean Christians, who adhered to the miaphysite theology formulated by Cyril of Alexandria, this definition seemed to compromise the organic unity of Christ's person, bordering on Nestorianism.

The rejection of the Chalcedonian creed by Aramean clergy transformed theological dissent into political defiance. The Byzantine administration viewed adherence to Chalcedonian orthodoxy as a primary marker of political loyalty. Consequently, West Syrian miaphysites were classified as heretics, initiating a multi-century policy of structural marginalization, intellectual suppression, and forced expulsions.

Part II: Imperial Suppression and Religious Resistance

The Enforcement of State Orthodoxy

Between 451 and 527 CE, successive Byzantine emperors utilized the power of the state to enforce Chalcedonian orthodoxy across the Eastern dioceses. Imperial authorities targeted key Aramean centers of learning and liturgical practice, imposing foreign Greek-speaking bishops upon traditional Syriac sees.

This period of intense conflict resulted in systemic hardships for the Aramean populations:

  • Expulsion of Clergy - Traditional Aramean bishops and theologians were systematically deposed and sent into exile.
  • Confiscation of Property - Major churches and monasteries were seized by imperial commissioners and handed over to Chalcedonian administrators.
  • Exodus to Rural Redoubts - Deprived of urban sanctuaries, Aramean communities retreated to mountainous redoubts, particularly the highlands of Tur Abdin, which became rugged strongholds of monastic resistance.

Part III: The Reign of Justinian and Empress Theodora’s Intervention

Sanctuary and Strategic Diplomacy

A crucial period of relief occurred during the reign of Emperor Justinian I (527-565 CE) due to the active intervention of his empress, Theodora (c. 500-548 CE). Raised in the East, Theodora was a committed supporter of the miaphysite cause and recognized the immense spiritual and cultural vitality of the Aramean communities.

Operating within the imperial palace, Theodora established an informal diplomatic and physical sanctuary. She converted portions of the Hormisdas Palace in Constantinople into a secure refuge, housing hundreds of exiled Aramean bishops, monks, and theologians. Through her direct financial support and political lobbying, she moderated Justinian's anti-miaphysite decrees, ensuring that the intellectual and spiritual leadership of the Aramean Church could survive and continue their theological work.

Part IV: Jacob Baradaeus and the Underground Church

The Re-establishment of a Parallel Hierarchy

Under the protection of Empress Theodora, a clandestine initiative was launched to secure the long-term survival of the Aramean Church. In 543 CE, Jacob Baradaeus (Ya'qub Burde'ana) was consecrated as a missionary bishop.

Traveling incognito across Syria, Mesopotamia, and Egypt - often disguised as a beggar to evade imperial arrest warrants - Jacob spent decades traveling on foot, ordaining tens of thousands of priests and consecrating dozens of bishops. His tireless efforts crystallized the underground Aramean network into an organized, permanent parallel ecclesiastical hierarchy, which would eventually be recognized as the Syriac Orthodox Church (popularly termed "Jacobite" by Byzantine authorities).

Part V: Aramean Diaspora and Geo-Political Flight

Migration to Sasanian and Arabian Frontiers

The persistent pressure of Byzantine administrative persecution spurred significant waves of Aramean migration outward from the imperial borders. Many Aramean scholars, monks, and families crossed the frontier into the Sasanian Persian Empire.

The Sasanian monarchs, eager to undermine Byzantine domestic solidarity, welcomed non-Chalcedonian Christian groups, granting them varying degrees of religious autonomy. Other Aramean communities migrated southward, integrating into the tribal confederations and urban centers of the Arabian Peninsula. These diasporic shifts spread literary Syriac and Christian Aramean culture far beyond the reach of Constantinople's legions.

Part VI: Twilight of Byzantine Rule and the Arab Conquest

The Shift in Imperial Hegemony

In the early 7th century, the devastating final war between the Byzantine Empire and Sasanian Persia (602-628 CE) left both empires exhausted and shattered the local infrastructure of the Levant. Following a brief Byzantine reoccupation, the rapid expansion of the Arab-Islamic forces culminated in the conquest of Damascus (635 CE) and Jerusalem (637 CE).

For many Aramean Christians, the collapse of Byzantine administration was met with a sense of political relief. While the new Islamic administration introduced distinct tax structures (the jizya) and secondary legal statuses, it brought an immediate end to the systematic, state-enforced religious persecutions that had plagued non-Chalcedonian Arameans for nearly two centuries.

Part VII: Legacy of the Byzantine Period

Endurance through Instability

Despite centuries of imperial hostility, the Byzantine era ultimately failed to absorb or eliminate Aramean cultural distinctiveness. Instead, the pressures of this era forced the Aramean communities to build exceptionally resilient local institutions. The preservation of the Aramaic (Syriac) language as a literary, liturgical, and scholarly medium ensured that West Syrian Christian identity remained linguistically and culturally independent of Hellenistic administrative control.
