Aram-Damascus was the most powerful and best-documented Aramean kingdom, centered on the city of Damascus in modern-day Syria.

Aram-Damascus rose to prominence in the 10th century BC and became the dominant Aramean state during the 9th and 8th centuries BC. It controlled territory stretching from the Anti-Lebanon mountains to the Hauran and at times into northern Israel. The kingdom was conquered by the Assyrian king Tiglath-Pileser III in 732 BC.

Sites

Archaeological evidence for Aram-Damascus is limited due to continuous habitation of Damascus, which has prevented large-scale excavation of Iron Age layers. However, related finds have been uncovered at:


Damascus - Capital city. Iron Age remains largely inaccessible beneath the modern city. Fragments of Aramaic-period walls and gates documented.
Tel Dan - Northern border site where the Tel Dan Stele was discovered, erected by an Aramean king (likely Hazael).
Bethsaida - Fortified city conquered by Aram-Damascus. Excavations revealed Iron Age gate complex and Aramean-style architecture.
Hazor - Destruction layers attributed to Hazael's campaigns in the late 9th century BC.

Architecture

While the core of Aramean Damascus remains unexcavated, evidence from related sites and Assyrian records indicates:


City fortifications - Massive walls and gates documented in Assyrian siege reliefs depicting the conquest of Damascus.
Bit-hilani palaces - Columned entrance halls characteristic of Aramean royal architecture, attested in Assyrian descriptions.
Temple of Hadad - The storm god Hadad (Ramman) was the chief deity. The temple site is believed to underlie the Umayyad Mosque.
