Arameans or Syriacs, also known as Native Syrians, are an ancient Semitic people from the Middle East, tracing their lineage back to Aram, son of Shem, found in the Table of Nations in Genesis 10 of the Bible.


The terms Aramu, Armi, and Arame appear for the first time in the Ebla tablets (c. 2300 BC), indicating a region in Upper Mesopotamia where it is believed early Arameans settled. The first explicit mention of the Arameans as a people occurs in inscriptions discovered at Kalah-Shergat belonging to Tiglath-Pileser I (c. 1100 BC).


Aramaic is the language of the Aramean people which once served as a lingua franca of the ancient Near East, replacing earlier Semitic languages in many administrative and cultural contexts. The language has developed through time and is generally divided into five historical phases: Old Aramaic, Official Aramaic, Middle Aramaic, Late Aramaic, and Modern Aramaic. It has also been divided into East and West varieties, and it has continued to exist in spoken and written forms into the present day. Aramaic is among the oldest well-attested Semitic languages and one of the world's longest continuously used recorded languages (c. 1000 BC), with historical development preceding and overlapping with languages such as Hebrew, Amharic, and Arabic.


The largest Aramean populations today exist in Syria, Iraq, USA, Lebanon, Sweden, Germany, and smaller populations are scattered around the globe. The total population is estimated to be around 5 million, excluding Saint Thomas Christians from India who belong to the Syriac ecclesiastical tradition but possess a distinct ethnic lineage.


The majority of the Aramean populations are Christians following either the West or East Syriac Churches. These institutions trace their roots back to the 1st century, marking these communities as some of the earliest to accept the Gospel.


Over time, various communities within the Syriac-speaking Near East developed distinct religious and cultural identities. Many of these groups emerged within the broader historical and linguistic environment of the ancient Aramean world, while later adopting different ecclesiastical, regional, or identity-based designations-including the Assyrian, Chaldean, Maronite, and Melkite traditions, as well as regional communities such as the Mhallami and historical classifications like the Sabians.
