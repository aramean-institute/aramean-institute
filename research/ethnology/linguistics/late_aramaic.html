Late Aramaic

Late Aramaic (c. 200 AD - 1200 AD) refers to the period when Aramaic had fully split into distinct eastern and western branches, each developing rich literary traditions. The western dialects were spoken in Palestine and Syria, while the eastern dialects dominated Mesopotamia. This period produced some of the most important religious and literary texts in Aramaic history.

Suryaya

Timeframe: c. 100 AD - 1400 AD
Also known as: Urhaya, Edessan, Classical Syriac
Spoken in: Edessa (Urhai), Upper Mesopotamia, and across the Near East
Key Texts: Peshitta (Bible translation), works of Ephrem, Aphrahat, Jacob of Serugh
Alphabet: ܐ ܒ ܓ ܕ ܗ ܘ ܙ ܚ ܛ ܝ ܟ ܠ ܡ ܢ ܣ ܥ ܦ ܨ ܩ ܪ ܫ ܬ

An Eastern Aramaic dialect that emerged in the region of Osroene, centered on the city of Edessa. It became the primary literary and liturgical language of Syriac Christianity, spreading across Mesopotamia, Syria, and as far as India and Central Asia. It reached its literary height prior to the Arab conquest in 636 AD, after which it gradually declined as a vernacular, being superseded by Arabic by around 1400 AD. It remains in liturgical use today.

Galilean

Timeframe: c. 200 AD - 700 AD
Spoken in: Galilee (northern Palestine)
Key Texts: Jerusalem Talmud, Palestinian Targumim, synagogue inscriptions
Alphabet: א ב ג ד ה ו ז ח ט י כ ל מ נ ס ע פ צ ק ר ש ת

A Western Aramaic dialect spoken by Jews in Galilee, particularly after the migration of rabbis from Judea following the destruction of the Temple in 70 AD. It is the language of the Jerusalem Talmud and much Palestinian rabbinic literature. It was distinguishable from the Judean dialect by its phonology, notably its treatment of gutturals. Gradually replaced by Arabic after the Muslim conquest in the 630s AD.

Palestinian

Timeframe: c. 400 AD - 1300 AD
Also known as: Christian Palestinian Aramaic, Melkite Aramaic
Spoken in: Palestine, Transjordan, and Sinai
Key Texts: Lectionaries, Gospel translations, hagiographic texts
Alphabet: ܐ ܒ ܓ ܕ ܗ ܘ ܙ ܚ ܛ ܝ ܟ ܠ ܡ ܢ ܣ ܥ ܦ ܨ ܩ ܪ ܫ ܬ

A Western Aramaic dialect used by the Melkite (Chalcedonian) Christian community, predominantly of Jewish descent. It is preserved in lectionaries, biblical translations, and liturgical texts. Despite using a script similar to Syriac Estrangela, it is linguistically a Western Aramaic dialect distinct from Syriac. It survived in limited use until around 1300 AD.

Samaritan

Timeframe: c. 100 AD - 1200 AD
Spoken in: Samaria (central Palestine, around Mount Gerizim)
Key Texts: Samaritan Targum of the Pentateuch, liturgical poetry
Alphabet: ࠀ ࠁ ࠂ ࠃ ࠄ ࠅ ࠆ ࠇ ࠈ ࠉ ࠊ ࠋ ࠌ ࠍ ࠎ ࠏ ࠐ ࠑ ࠒ ࠓ ࠔ ࠕ

A Western Aramaic dialect used by the Samaritan community for religious and literary purposes. It is primarily known from the Samaritan Targum and liturgical compositions. In form it resembles the Aramaic of the Jewish Targumim but is written in the distinctive Samaritan script. It became extinct as a spoken language between 1000 AD and 1200 AD, replaced by Palestinian Arabic.

Talmudic

Timeframe: c. 300 AD - 1100 AD
Also known as: Jewish Babylonian Aramaic
Spoken in: Lower Mesopotamia (Babylonia, modern southern Iraq)
Key Texts: Babylonian Talmud
Alphabet: א ב ג ד ה ו ז ח ט י כ ל מ נ ס ע פ צ ק ר ש ת

An Eastern Aramaic dialect used by Jewish scholars in Babylonia. It is the language of the Babylonian Talmud, the most influential work of rabbinic literature, compiled between 200 AD and 500 AD and continued as a literary language until around 1100 AD. It differs significantly from the Western Palestinian Jewish dialects in phonology, morphology, and vocabulary.

Mandaic

Timeframe: c. 200 AD
Spoken in: Southern Mesopotamia (modern southern Iraq and Khuzestan, Iran)
Key Texts: Ginza Rabba, Book of John, incantation bowls
Alphabet: 𐫀 𐫁 𐫂 𐫃 𐫄 𐫅 𐫆 𐫇 𐫈 𐫉 𐫊 𐫋 𐫌 𐫍 𐫎 𐫏 𐫐 𐫑 𐫒 𐫓 𐫔 𐫕

An Eastern Aramaic dialect used by the Mandaean Gnostic community. Its roots trace back to the Aramaic of Babylonia from the early Parthian period. It developed a unique alphabet, likely evolved between 200 AD and 700 AD from a cursive form of Aramaic. Classical Mandaic is used in religious texts, while a modern descendant (Neo-Mandaic) is still spoken by a small community in Khuzestan, Iran.
