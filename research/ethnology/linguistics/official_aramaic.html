Official Aramaic, also known as Imperial Aramaic (c. 700 BC - 300 BC), represents the period when the language was standardized for international diplomacy. Initially adopted by the Neo-Assyrian and Neo-Babylonian administrations, it later became the primary administrative tongue of the Achaemenid Persian Empire, stretching from Egypt to the Indus Valley.

Aramaya (Neo-Assyrian)

Timeframe: c. 700 BC - 600 BC
Usage: Western Provincial Administration
Significance: The Proto-Standard

The foundational phase where Aramaic began to replace Akkadian as the practical language of the Assyrian Empire. It was during this period that the cursive script was refined for use on papyrus and parchment rather than just stone or clay.

Achaemenid (Standard Imperial)

Timeframe: c. 539 BC - 330 BC
Usage: Imperial Chancellery / Diplomacy
Key Source: Persepolis Fortification Archive

The most uniform stage of the language. Under Persian rule, scribes used a "prestige" form of Aramaic that was identical across all satrapies, ensuring that a decree from the King could be read by officials thousands of miles apart.

Biblical Aramaic

Timeframe: c. 5th - 2nd Century BC
Usage: Literary / Religious
Key Source: Books of Ezra and Daniel

The variety of Official Aramaic preserved in the Hebrew Bible. While it adheres to the Imperial standard, it shows the influence of Jewish literary culture and is the primary example of how the language was used for sacred storytelling.

Elephantine (Egyptian Corpus)

Timeframe: c. 5th Century BC
Usage: Legal / Military / Daily Life
Key Source: Elephantine Papyri

A massive collection of documents from a Jewish garrison in Egypt. These records provide the best evidence for how the Official standard was used for marriage contracts, private business, and local military administration.

Late Imperial (Transitional)

Timeframe: c. 300 BC - 200 BC
Usage: Post-Achaemenid Administration
Status: Regional Fragmentation

Following Alexander the Great’s conquest, the central authority that enforced the Aramaic standard vanished. This period marks the "break" where local dialects began to re-emerge, leading to the distinct scripts of the Middle Aramaic period.
