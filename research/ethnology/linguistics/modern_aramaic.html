Modern Aramaic

Modern Aramaic or Neo-Aramaic (c. 1200 AD - present) refers to the living varieties of Aramaic still spoken today by Aramean and Jewish communities. These languages evolved from the Late Aramaic dialects during the late medieval period and continue as endangered vernacular languages in the Middle East and in diaspora communities worldwide.

Suryoyo

Timeframe: c. 200 AD - present (as liturgical and literary language)
Also known as: Kthobonoyo, Classical Syriac, Leššono Kthobonoyo
Spoken in: Liturgical use across Syriac churches worldwide
Key Texts: Peshitta, liturgical hymns, modern ecclesiastical writings
Alphabet: ܐ ܒ ܓ ܕ ܗ ܘ ܙ ܚ ܛ ܝ ܟ ܠ ܡ ܢ ܣ ܥ ܦ ܨ ܩ ܪ ܫ ܬ

The literary and liturgical form of Syriac, preserved as the formal written language of Syriac Christianity. It is used in church services, theological education, and formal writing by both Eastern and Western Syriac traditions. While no longer a spoken vernacular, it remains actively used in liturgy and is taught in schools and seminaries. It serves as the literary standard alongside the spoken modern varieties.

Surayt

Timeframe: c. 1200 AD - present
Also known as: Turoyo
Spoken in: Tur Abdin (southeastern Turkey), northeastern Syria, and diaspora (Sweden, Germany, Netherlands)
Speakers: ~250,000
Key Texts: Oral literature, modern written works (earliest texts from the late 1800s AD)
Alphabet: ܐ ܒ ܓ ܕ ܗ ܘ ܙ ܚ ܛ ܝ ܟ ܠ ܡ ܢ ܣ ܥ ܦ ܨ ܩ ܪ ܫ ܬ

A Central Neo-Aramaic language and the spoken vernacular of the Syriac Christian community in the Tur Abdin region. The dominant dialect is that of Midyat. Today most speakers live in European diaspora communities, particularly in Sweden and Germany, due to displacement from their homeland during the Sayfo (1915) and later conflicts. It is distinct from the literary Suryoyo but closely related to it historically.

Mlahso

Timeframe: c. 1200 AD - 1998 AD (extinct)
Also known as: Mlaḥsô
Spoken in: Villages of Mlahso and Ansha near Diyarbakir (southeastern Turkey), later Qamishli (Syria)
Speakers: Extinct (last speaker died in 1998)
Alphabet: ܐ ܒ ܓ ܕ ܗ ܘ ܙ ܚ ܛ ܝ ܟ ܠ ܡ ܢ ܣ ܥ ܦ ܨ ܩ ܪ ܫ ܬ

A Central Neo-Aramaic language closely related to Surayt/Turoyo but distinct enough to be considered a separate variety. It was spoken by Syriac Orthodox Christians in villages about 100 km northwest of Tur Abdin. The communities were destroyed during the Sayfo in 1915, and surviving speakers resettled in Qamishli, Syria. The last known speaker died in 1998, making the language extinct.

Sureth

Timeframe: c. 1200 AD - present
Also known as: Suret, Assyrian Neo-Aramaic, Chaldean Neo-Aramaic
Spoken in: Northern Iraq (Nineveh Plains), southeastern Turkey, northwestern Iran (Urmia), northeastern Syria, and diaspora (USA, Australia, Sweden)
Speakers: ~220,000
Key Texts: Modern literature, newspapers, Bible translations
Alphabet: ܐ ܒ ܓ ܕ ܗ ܘ ܙ ܚ ܛ ܝ ܟ ܠ ܡ ܢ ܣ ܥ ܦ ܨ ܩ ܪ ܫ ܬ

The main group of Northeastern Neo-Aramaic varieties spoken by East Arameans. It encompasses several dialects including Assyrian Neo-Aramaic and Chaldean Neo-Aramaic. Before World War I, it was spoken as a vernacular between the Tigris and Lake Urmia, stretching north to Lake Van and south to Mosul and Kirkuk. Speakers were heavily displaced during the Sayfo (1915) and later conflicts in Iraq. It is written in the Madnhaya (Eastern) form of the Syriac script.

Bohtan

Timeframe: c. 1200 AD - present (endangered)
Also known as: Bohtan Neo-Aramaic
Spoken in: Originally Bohtan plain (southeastern Turkey), now Gardabani (Georgia), Göygöl and Ağstafa (Azerbaijan)
Speakers: Small community in the Caucasus
Alphabet: ܐ ܒ ܓ ܕ ܗ ܘ ܙ ܚ ܛ ܝ ܟ ܠ ܡ ܢ ܣ ܥ ܦ ܨ ܩ ܪ ܫ ܬ

A Northeastern Neo-Aramaic dialect originally spoken by East Arameans on the plain of Bohtan in the Ottoman Empire. Speakers were displaced during the Sayfo in 1915 and settled in Gardabani near Rustavi in Georgia, and in parts of Azerbaijan. It is closely related to Hertevin. The community has maintained the language in isolation from other Aramaic-speaking groups for over a century.

Hertevin

Timeframe: c. 1200 AD - present (nearly extinct)
Also known as: Hértevin
Spoken in: Originally villages in Siirt Province (southeastern Turkey), now scattered in diaspora
Speakers: A few speakers remain in Turkey, others scattered in the West
Alphabet: ܐ ܒ ܓ ܕ ܗ ܘ ܙ ܚ ܛ ܝ ܟ ܠ ܡ ܢ ܣ ܥ ܦ ܨ ܩ ܪ ܫ ܬ

A Northeastern Neo-Aramaic dialect originally spoken by Chaldean Catholics in a cluster of villages in Siirt Province, southeastern Turkey. Its closest relative is Bohtan Neo-Aramaic. Speakers have emigrated mostly to the West and are now scattered and isolated from one another. The language is nearly extinct.

Senaya

Timeframe: c. 1200 AD - present (nearly extinct)
Also known as: Sanandaj Christian Neo-Aramaic
Spoken in: Originally Sanandaj (western Iran), now California (USA) and Tehran
Speakers: Small number of adults, not transmitted to children
Alphabet: ܐ ܒ ܓ ܕ ܗ ܘ ܙ ܚ ܛ ܝ ܟ ܠ ܡ ܢ ܣ ܥ ܦ ܨ ܩ ܪ ܫ ܬ

A Northeastern Neo-Aramaic dialect originally spoken by Christians in Sanandaj, Iran. It developed in geographic isolation at the southeastern periphery of the NENA-speaking area, making it quite distinct from other dialects. A separate Jewish variety (Hulaulá) was spoken in the same city. Most speakers now live in California, with a few families remaining in Tehran.

Koy Sanjaq Surat

Timeframe: c. 1200 AD - present (endangered)
Also known as: Koy Sanjaq Christian Neo-Aramaic
Spoken in: Koy Sanjaq, Erbil Governorate (northern Iraq)
Speakers: Small and decreasing
Alphabet: ܐ ܒ ܓ ܕ ܗ ܘ ܙ ܚ ܛ ܝ ܟ ܠ ܡ ܢ ܣ ܥ ܦ ܨ ܩ ܪ ܫ ܬ

A Northeastern Neo-Aramaic variety spoken by East Arameans in the town of Koy Sanjaq in northern Iraq. A separate Jewish variety was also spoken in the same town until 1951, when the entire Jewish community emigrated to Israel. The Christian dialect has lingered on in the town but is endangered, with decreasing numbers of young speakers.

Suryon

Timeframe: c. 1200 AD - present (critically endangered)
Also known as: Ma'luli, Western Neo-Aramaic
Spoken in: Maaloula, Jubb'adin, and Bakh'a (villages near Damascus, Syria)
Speakers: ~30 (severely endangered)
Alphabet: Arabic script and Latin transcription

The sole surviving remnant of the Western Aramaic branch. It is spoken by both Christian and Muslim Arameans in three small villages about 60 km northeast of Damascus. It descends from Damascene Aramaic and is the only living Western Aramaic language, making it linguistically distinct from all other modern varieties which are Eastern Aramaic. The Syrian Civil War (2011-present) further endangered the language as many speakers were displaced.

Neo-Mandaic

Timeframe: c. 1200 AD - present (critically endangered)
Also known as: Modern Mandaic
Spoken in: Ahvaz and Khorramshahr (Khuzestan, Iran), and diaspora
Speakers: ~100-300
Alphabet: 𐫀 𐫁 𐫂 𐫃 𐫄 𐫅 𐫆 𐫇 𐫈 𐫉 𐫊 𐫋 𐫌 𐫍 𐫎 𐫏 𐫐 𐫑 𐫒 𐫓 𐫔 𐫕

The modern descendant of Classical Mandaic, spoken by the Mandaean religious community. It is the only surviving form of any classical literary Aramaic dialect to continue as a spoken language. Severely endangered, it is spoken by possibly as few as 100-200 people in Iran and in the Mandaean diaspora. It retains the unique Mandaic alphabet inherited from the classical period.

Jewish Neo-Aramaic

The Jewish Neo-Aramaic languages are a group of Northeastern Neo-Aramaic varieties historically spoken by Jewish communities in northern Iraq and western Iran. They developed alongside but separately from the Christian NENA dialects (Sureth), and are not mutually intelligible with them. Following the mass emigration of Jews from the Muslim world in the 1950s, most speakers relocated to Israel, where the languages face extinction as younger generations shift to Hebrew.

Lishana Deni

Timeframe: c. 1200 AD - present (nearly extinct)
Also known as: Zakho Jewish Neo-Aramaic
Spoken in: Zakho and surrounding region (northern Iraq / southeastern Turkey), now Israel
Speakers: Elderly speakers only, in Israel
Alphabet: א ב ג ד ה ו ז ח ט י כ ל מ נ ס ע פ צ ק ר ש ת

A Northeastern Neo-Aramaic dialect originally spoken by Jews in the Zakho region of northern Iraq and southeastern Turkey. The name means "our language." Following the exodus of Jews from Iraq in the early 1950s, most speakers settled in Jerusalem and surrounding areas in Israel. The language is now spoken only by elderly community members.

Lishanid Noshan

Timeframe: c. 1200 AD - present (nearly extinct)
Also known as: Inter-Zab Jewish Neo-Aramaic, Arbil Jewish Neo-Aramaic
Spoken in: Arbil/Hewler region between the Great Zab and Little Zab rivers (northern Iraq), Cizre and Hakkari (Turkey), now Israel
Speakers: Elderly speakers only, in Israel
Alphabet: א ב ג ד ה ו ז ח ט י כ ל מ נ ס ע פ צ ק ר ש ת

A Northeastern Neo-Aramaic dialect originally spoken by Jews in and around Arbil in northern Iraq. The name means "our tongue." For generations, the Arbil Jews maintained a distinct cultural identity amid Aramean, Kurdish, and Arab influences, working as traders, craftsmen, and dyers. Most speakers emigrated to Israel in the 1950s.

Hulaulá

Timeframe: c. 1200 AD - present (nearly extinct)
Also known as: Trans-Zab Jewish Neo-Aramaic, Galiglu
Spoken in: Western Iran and easternmost northern Iraq (Sulemaniyya, Halabja, Sanandaj), now Israel
Speakers: Elderly speakers only, in Israel
Alphabet: א ב ג ד ה ו ז ח ט י כ ל מ נ ס ע פ צ ק ר ש ת

A group of related Northeastern Neo-Aramaic dialects originally spoken by Jews in western Iran and the easternmost parts of northern Iraq. The name means "Jewish" in the dialect. It is heavily influenced by Kurdish and Persian. Speakers sometimes call it Lishana Noshan or Lishana Akhni ("our language"). Most speakers emigrated to Israel in the 1950s.

Lishán Didán

Timeframe: c. 1200 AD - present (critically endangered)
Also known as: Jewish Azerbaijani Neo-Aramaic, Urmia Jewish Neo-Aramaic
Spoken in: Urmia, Salmas, and Naghade (northwestern Iran / Iranian Azerbaijan), eastern Turkey (Yüksekova, Hakkari), now Israel
Speakers: ~5,000 (mostly over 50 years old)
Alphabet: א ב ג ד ה ו ז ח ט י כ ל מ נ ס ע פ צ ק ר ש ת

A Northeastern Neo-Aramaic dialect originally spoken by Jews in the Urmia region of northwestern Iran and parts of eastern Turkey. The name means "our language." The community is also known as Nash Didan ("our people"). Most speakers fled to Israel after World War II. The language faces extinction within decades as younger generations shift to Hebrew.

Barzani

Timeframe: c. 1200 AD - present (effectively extinct)
Also known as: Barzani Jewish Neo-Aramaic
Spoken in: Barzan and villages near Akre (northern Iraq), now Israel
Speakers: ~20 elderly speakers (as of 2004), effectively extinct
Alphabet: א ב ג ד ה ו ז ח ט י כ ל מ נ ס ע פ צ ק ר ש ת

A Northeastern Neo-Aramaic dialect originally spoken by Jews in three villages near Akre in northern Iraq. Three sub-dialects were documented: Barzan, Shahe, and Bejil (the latter already extinct). The community emigrated to Israel in the 1950s. The first language of remaining speakers is now Hebrew or Kurdish, making the language effectively extinct.

Betanure

Timeframe: c. 1200 AD - present (critically endangered)
Also known as: Betanure Jewish Neo-Aramaic
Spoken in: Village of Betanure (northern Iraq), now Israel
Speakers: ~36 (as of 2008)
Alphabet: א ב ג ד ה ו ז ח ט י כ ל מ נ ס ע פ צ ק ר ש ת

Among the rarest and most seriously endangered varieties of Aramaic. It is also one of the most linguistically conservative of all Jewish Neo-Aramaic languages. In the 1940s it was spoken by seventeen large families in the village of Betanure. The entire community migrated to Israel in 1951. The dialect has since faced erosion from Hebrew and from other Neo-Aramaic varieties spoken in Israel.
