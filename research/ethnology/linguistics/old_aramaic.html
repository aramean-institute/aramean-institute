Old Aramaic or Ancient Aramaic refers to the earliest attested phase of the Aramaic language. It is primarily documented through the bilingual inscription discovered in 1979 at Tell Fekheriyah, which serves as a critical linguistic bridge between Aramaic and Akkadian. The language was first spoken by the Arameans, an ethnic group traditionally associated with Aram, son of Shem, from whom the language derives its name. While ancestral tribes appear earlier in historical narratives, the archaeological record officially "starts the clock" for Old Aramaic at c. 1100 BC.

Timeframe: c. 1100 BC - 700 BC

Aram (Central/Standard)

c. 1100 BC - 750 BC

The dominant dialect of the southern Syrian city-states, famously preserved on the Tel Dan Stele.

Samalian

c. 900 BC - 700 BC

An archaic, isolated dialect from the Kingdom of Sam'al (Zincirli) featuring unique grammatical structures that predate the standardization of the language.

Fekheriyan (Eastern)

c. 850 BC - 800 BC

Representing the Eastern dialectal branch found at Gozan; it highlights early contact and script-sharing between Aramean and Neo-Assyrian cultures.

Arpad / Sfire (Late Old Aramaic)

c. 750 BC - 700 BC

The final, most sophisticated phase of Old Aramaic, primarily utilized in legal treaties and high-level international diplomacy prior to the rise of Imperial Aramaic.
