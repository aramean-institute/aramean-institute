Old Aramaic (or Ancient Aramaic) refers to the earliest attested phase of the Aramaic language - the oldest known form of Aramaic. It was first spoken by the Arameans, a Semitic people traditionally associated with Aram, son of Shem, from whom the language takes its name. The language is primarily known from monumental inscriptions written in an early Phoenician-derived alphabetic script. While Aramean groups appear in Assyrian historical records from the late 1200s-1100s BC, the archaeological record of written Aramaic properly begins around the late 1100s-1000s BC, with some of the earliest inscriptions associated with Aramean kingdoms such as Bit-Agusi and Sam'al. One of the most important early texts is the bilingual inscription discovered in 1979 at Tell Fekheriye in northeastern Syria, which serves as a major linguistic bridge between early Aramaic and Assyrian Akkadian.

Proto-Aram

Timeframe: c. 1100 BC - 900 BC
Spoken in: Southern and Central Syria (Damascus region and the Levant)
Key Inscription: None (spoken-only phase)

This represents the very earliest form of Aramaic as a distinct language, spoken by Aramean tribes and city-states after the Late Bronze Age collapse. No substantial written texts survive from this period. It is the linguistic ancestor of all later Aramaic varieties.

Aram (Central/Standard)

Timeframe: c. 850 BC - 750 BC
Spoken in: Southern Syria (Damascus) and Northern Israel (Galilee / Golan)
Key Inscription: Tel Dan Stele

The dominant western dialect of the major Aramean city-states. It contains the earliest extra-biblical reference to the "House of David" (bytdwd) and represents the core variety closest to what later became standardized.

Samalian

Timeframe: c. 900 BC - 700 BC
Spoken in: Southeastern Turkey (Zincirli / Ancient Sam'al)
Key Inscription: Hadad & Panamuwa Stelae

An archaic and somewhat isolated dialect (sometimes treated as a peripheral or transitional variety with Canaanite-like features). It preserves very old grammatical structures, lacks the definite article found in other Old Aramaic texts, and shows unique morphological traits.

Fekheriyan

Timeframe: c. 850 BC - 800 BC
Spoken in: Northeastern Syria (Khabur River Valley / Gozan)
Key Inscription: Tell Fekheriyah Statue

One of the earliest substantial Aramaic texts. It demonstrates early cultural and linguistic contact with the Neo-Assyrian Empire, features early matres lectionis for vowels, and highlights the eastern branch of the language.

Arpad

Timeframe: c. 750 BC - 700 BC
Spoken in: Northern Syria (Aleppo region / Tell Rifa'at)
Key Inscription: Sfire Stelae

The most advanced and grammatically sophisticated stage before the Imperial period. These detailed treaty inscriptions reflect developed diplomatic and legal language, showing increasing standardization across dialects.
