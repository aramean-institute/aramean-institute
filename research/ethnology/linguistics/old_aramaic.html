Old Aramaic or Ancient Aramaic refers to the earliest attested phase of the Aramaic language. It is primarily documented through the bilingual inscription discovered in 1979 at Tell Fekheriyah, which serves as a critical linguistic bridge between Aramaic and Akkadian. The language was first spoken by the Arameans, an ethnic group traditionally associated with Aram, son of Shem, from whom the language derives its name. While ancestral tribes appear earlier in historical narratives, the archaeological record officially "starts the clock" for Old Aramaic at c. 1100 BC.


Aram (Central/Standard)

Timeframe: c. 1100 BC - 750 BC
Spoken in: Southern Syria (Damascus) and Northern Israel (Galilee / Golan)
Key Inscription: Tel Dan Stele

The dominant dialect of the southern Syrian city-states; it contains the earliest extra-biblical reference to the "House of David."

Samalian

Timeframe: c. 900 BC - 700 BC
Spoken in: Southeastern Turkey (Zincirli / Ancient Sam'al)
Key Inscription: Hadad & Panamuwa Stelae

An archaic, isolated dialect from the Kingdom of Sam'al featuring unique grammatical structures that predate the standardization of the language.

Fekheriyan

Timeframe: c. 850 BC - 800 BC
Spoken in: Northeastern Syria (Khabur River Valley / Gozan)
Key Inscription: Tell Fekheriyah Statue

Representing the Eastern branch; it highlights early contact and script-sharing between Aramean and Neo-Assyrian cultures.

Arpad

Timeframe: c. 750 BC - 700 BC
Spoken in: Northern Syria (Aleppo region / Tell Rifa'at)
Key Inscription: Sfire Stelae

The Late Old Aramaic phase; the most sophisticated stage of the language before the Persian era, primarily used in legal treaties and diplomacy.
