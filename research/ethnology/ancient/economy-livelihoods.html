Ancient Economy and Livelihoods

  4. Economy and Livelihoods

Period scope: Iron Age and ancient contexts. Economic practices differed by kingdom, settlement, ecology, household status, and access to imperial or regional markets.

Work and Subsistence

Ancient Aramean livelihoods combined cultivation, herding, craft production, transport, and exchange. The balance between these activities changed across settled towns, villages, river valleys, upland communities, and mobile or semi-mobile pastoral settings.

Agriculture and Pastoralism

  • Cereal cultivation: Wheat and barley supported household consumption, taxation, and redistribution.
  • Garden and orchard production: Grapes, olives, dates, figs, legumes, and vegetables were cultivated where water and soils permitted.
  • Herding: Sheep and goats supplied meat, milk, wool, hides, and exchange goods.
  • Animal traction and transport: Donkeys, cattle, horses, and camels had different agricultural, military, and transport roles.

Crafts and Household Production

Households produced or commissioned textiles, pottery, leather goods, tools, food products, and building materials. Some specialists worked for courts, temples, merchants, or urban markets. The surviving evidence is uneven, so occupational labels should not be read as a complete census of ancient work.

  • Spinning, weaving, dyeing, and processing wool or plant fibers
  • Pottery manufacture and repair of storage and cooking vessels
  • Metalworking in bronze and iron, including tools and weapons
  • Stone, wood, and leather working for buildings, transport, and household use

Trade and Exchange

Markets and longer-distance exchange connected local producers with neighboring regions. Grain, wine, oil, livestock, textiles, metals, timber, and crafted goods could move through roads, river routes, and political networks. Merchants and officials operated within changing systems of tribute, taxation, gift exchange, and market commerce.

Land, Labor, and Redistribution

Landholding and access to water were not distributed equally. Royal and temple institutions could organize labor, collect agricultural produce, and redistribute resources, while village households and pastoral groups maintained their own production strategies. Terms such as “peasant,” “slave,” or “estate” must be applied carefully because their meaning and evidence vary by period and source.

Occupational and Social Difference

  • Farmers, herders, and agricultural laborers
  • Merchants, carriers, and local traders
  • Craft specialists and builders
  • Scribes, administrators, soldiers, and religious personnel
  • Dependent, captive, or otherwise unfree laborers in contexts where the sources attest them

Regional Variation

Damascus, the Euphrates and Khabur regions, the Levantine uplands, and other Aramean-associated areas did not share one economic system. Climate, political authority, urbanization, warfare, and imperial incorporation shaped local opportunities and burdens. Historical changes over time belong in History; this page emphasizes how people worked and sustained households within those conditions.
