The Simele Massacre was a campaign of mass killings, forced displacement, and destruction against Aramean communities in northern Iraq in August 1933. It took place during the political crisis that followed the end of the British Mandate and Iraq’s admission to the League of Nations.

The event is commonly called the Simele Massacre, although the violence affected Simele and surrounding villages. Some sources and communities use different spellings for the place name. This article uses Aramean as the principal community term while recognizing that historical records may use other names.

Iraq after the Ottoman Empire

The new Iraqi state inherited borders and political tensions from the collapse of the Ottoman Empire and the British Mandate. Northern Iraq included diverse populations, and the Mosul region had experienced war, displacement, and refugee movements since the First World War. Many Aramean families who had survived the destruction of their communities in the late Ottoman period were living in Iraq or seeking safety there [1] [2].


Iraq became independent and entered the League of Nations in 1932. The end of the mandate increased the government’s concern about territorial control, foreign influence, and armed opposition. Aramean requests for protection and communal security were increasingly treated by officials as a political and security problem. The precise motives behind the later campaign remain debated, but official suspicion and the weakness of minority protections created conditions in which violence could spread [1] [3] [4].

The Crisis of 1933

During the summer of 1933, disputes over the status and security of Aramean communities intensified. Some armed Arameans attempted to cross into French-mandated Syria in search of protection. After pressure forced many to return to Iraq, the government treated the movement as a challenge to state authority. A clash at Dairabun between Aramean fighters and Iraqi forces was followed by a wider military operation in the northern districts [1] [3].


The confrontation was then used to justify collective punishment. Instead of limiting military action to armed opponents, Iraqi forces moved against civilian settlements. The transition from a political and military dispute to attacks on villages is central to understanding the massacre and the failure of the Iraqi state to protect its Aramean population.

The Massacre in Simele and Surrounding Villages

Beginning in early August 1933, Iraqi army units under General Bakr Sidqi entered Simele and nearby settlements. Reports describe the killing of civilians, including women and children, as well as looting, the burning of homes, and the destruction or seizure of community property. Violence was not limited to one village; families were attacked or expelled across a wider area of northern Iraq [1] [2] [3].


The principal responsibility lay with the Iraqi state and military forces that organized and carried out the operations. Local auxiliaries, including some Kurdish and Arab armed groups, also participated in attacks or property seizures in certain places. This documented participation should not be turned into collective blame against all Kurds, Arabs, or other communities; individual responses varied, and some people protected Aramean neighbors or helped survivors escape.

Losses, Displacement, and Destruction

Casualty estimates vary because records were incomplete, survivors were displaced, and studies use different geographic boundaries. Most accounts describe the deaths as numbering in the thousands, although no single figure should be presented as exact without identifying its source and method. The losses included not only those killed but also families separated, homes destroyed, property confiscated, and villages emptied [1] [2] [3].


Survivors fled toward Mosul, Dohuk, Zakho, Syria, and other parts of Iraq. Displacement weakened village networks and separated families from churches, schools, cemeteries, and agricultural land. The massacre therefore caused both immediate loss of life and a longer-term rupture in the presence of Aramean communities in northern Iraq.

The British and International Response

British officials and diplomats recorded the violence and its consequences, but the international response did not produce effective accountability for the perpetrators. British policy was shaped by its treaty relationship with Iraq and by a wider desire to preserve the new state’s stability. The absence of meaningful protection or justice deepened the insecurity of Aramean survivors and contributed to further emigration [1] [2] [4].


The massacre also demonstrated the limits of minority guarantees after the end of the mandate. Legal independence did not ensure equal security for all communities, and the Iraqi government’s treatment of Arameans became an important part of later debates about citizenship, protection, and communal rights.

Memory and Historical Significance

The Simele Massacre remains a central event in modern Aramean historical memory. Aramean communities commemorate the victims and preserve the names of villages, families, clergy, teachers, and other civilians who were killed or displaced. The date of the massacre is also remembered in Aramean communities as a day of mourning for the victims of modern persecution.


Simele was not the same event as Seyfo, which took place primarily during the First World War under Ottoman rule. The two episodes are connected in community memory because many survivors of the earlier destruction had sought refuge in Iraq, where their safety remained uncertain. Studying Simele as a distinct 1933 event clarifies the responsibility of the Iraqi state and the particular political conditions of the period.

Conclusion

The Simele Massacre was a major campaign of violence against Aramean civilians in northern Iraq. Iraqi state and military forces carried the principal responsibility, while some local armed groups also participated. The killings, displacement, and destruction exposed the vulnerability of Aramean communities after Iraq’s independence and left a lasting mark on Aramean memory, migration, and demands for recognition and protection.
