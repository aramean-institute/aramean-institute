The Aramaic of Tur Abdin is the Aramaic of Urhoy (Edessa). "Aramaya" became "Suryaya" and "Suryaya" became "Suryoyo."

The Aramaic of Edessa

The Syriac language is a literary dialect of Aramaic that originated in the city of Urhoy (Edessa, modern Urfa) and its surrounding region [1]. It became the most important literary dialect of Late Aramaic, serving as the language of scholarship, liturgy, and literature throughout Mesopotamia and the Levant.


The term "Syriac" (Suryaya) is itself a Greek-derived name for "Aramaic" (Aramaya). According to the scholar Theodor Noldeke, "Syrian" (Syria) was the term the Greeks used for the Arameans they encountered in Mesopotamia [2]. In many Syriac writers, the terms "Aramaya" and "Suryaya" are used as synonyms when referring to the language [3].

From Aramaya to Suryoyo

The linguistic shift from "Aramaya" to "Suryaya" to "Suryoyo" reflects phonological development. "Suryaya" is the Classical Syriac (Eastern) pronunciation, while "Suryoyo" is the Western Syriac pronunciation used by the communities of Tur Abdin. Both refer to the same Aramaic language.


The dialect spoken today in Tur Abdin, known as Surayt or Turoyo, is a Modern Aramaic language traditionally spoken by the Syriac Christian community in the region [4]. It descends directly from the Aramaic literary tradition of Edessa and preserves many archaic features of the language.

Tur Abdin

Tur Abdin (Syriac: "Mountain of the Servants [of God]") is a mountainous region in southeastern Turkey, historically part of upper Mesopotamia. It has been a center of Syriac Orthodox Christianity and Aramaic language preservation for nearly two millennia. The monasteries and villages of Tur Abdin maintained the Aramaic language through centuries of political change, making it one of the last regions where Aramaic continues to be spoken as a living language.


Classical Syriac (Suryoyo) remains in active use as the liturgical language of the Syriac Orthodox Church, used in prayer, hymns, and scripture readings to this day.
