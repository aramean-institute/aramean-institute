Seyfo, also called Sayfo, is the name used by many Arameans for the mass violence, deportation, starvation, and destruction inflicted on Christian populations in the Ottoman Empire during the First World War, especially between 1915 and 1918.

The word Sayfo is commonly translated as “sword” in Aramaic. The term expresses the memory of a period in which communities were attacked, displaced, and deprived of their homes, churches, schools, manuscripts, and livelihoods. This article uses Aramean as the principal name for the communities discussed, while recognizing that historical records and modern communities may use different names.

The Ottoman and Wartime Setting

The violence took place within the political and military crisis of the late Ottoman Empire. Eastern Anatolia and northern Mesopotamia were home to diverse Christian and Muslim communities, including Armenians, Arameans, Kurds, Arabs, and Turks. World War I intensified state suspicion, forced mobilization, food shortages, and armed conflict along the Ottoman–Russian frontier [1] [3].


The same wartime period also saw mass violence against other Christian populations, including Pontic Greeks and Armenians. The Pontic Greek genocide was concentrated largely in the Black Sea region and remains a related but distinct history; it should not be merged with the Aramean experience or used to obscure the particular histories of different communities.


Ottoman authorities disarmed or removed parts of the Christian population, while deportation, requisition, imprisonment, and killing were carried out through a combination of state institutions, military units, irregular forces, and local actors. The pattern varied by province and town. Some local Kurdish, Arab, and Turkish individuals participated in attacks or property seizures, while others protected neighbors, helped people escape, or refused orders. A single description of all Turks or all Kurds therefore obscures the documented differences between local actors and regions [1] [2].


The violence involved several levels of responsibility. Ottoman state authorities, including provincial officials, soldiers, gendarmes, and irregular forces, organized or enabled deportations, killings, and property seizures. In some regions, local Kurdish, Arab, and Turkish tribal leaders and armed groups also participated in attacks against Aramean communities. Kurdish participation was significant in particular areas, but it was not uniform: some Kurdish individuals and leaders protected Arameans, helped families escape, or refused to participate. Responsibility should therefore be assigned to documented authorities and perpetrators rather than collectively to all Kurds, Turks, Arabs, or other communities [1] [2].


The coordinated use of deportation, killing, starvation, and dispossession indicates that Ottoman wartime authorities and allied forces sought to remove Christian communities from strategic areas and, in many places, accepted or facilitated their destruction. Many Aramean scholars and commemorative traditions therefore describe Seyfo as an exterminatory campaign or genocide. Responsibility and intent nevertheless need to be described precisely: “Ottoman authorities” does not mean that every Ottoman official, soldier, or local inhabitant participated in the same way, and local patterns differed across the affected regions [1] [2] [5].

Communities and Regions Affected

Seyfo affected Aramean communities in several parts of the Ottoman Empire, including Hakkari, Diyarbekir, Mardin, Tur Abdin, and the districts around Midyat and Şırnak. Villages and towns experienced different combinations of massacre, flight, deportation, famine, and forced conversion. In some places, survivors gathered in monasteries or defended themselves; in others, entire settlements were emptied and their houses, fields, and religious institutions were taken over.


Violence in the Urmia region of northwestern Iran is often discussed together with Seyfo because the same war, refugee movements, and cross-border military campaigns connected the communities. Ismail Agha Simko, leader of the Şikak Kurdish tribal confederation, was involved in attacks against Christian populations in and around Urmia. His actions belong to this connected Iranian theater, whose political setting was different from the Ottoman provincial history; they should not be treated as the sole cause of Seyfo or as evidence that all Kurdish communities participated in the massacres. Separating related theaters makes the chronology and responsibility of each episode clearer [1] [2].

Resistance, Flight, and Protection

Christian communities did not respond in one uniform way. Some attempted to negotiate with local officials or tribal leaders, while others fled toward safer towns, monasteries, Russian-held areas, or neighboring countries. Armed resistance occurred in several localities, including the defense of villages in the Tur Abdin region. These actions could protect people temporarily, but they also brought prolonged siege, hunger, and further displacement.


Accounts of protection are also part of the history. Muslim neighbors, tribal leaders, officials, clergy, and foreign workers sometimes sheltered families, arranged escape, or returned children and property. Such cases do not erase the broader pattern of persecution, but they are important for an accurate account of individual choices and local relationships [1] [2].

Human and Cultural Consequences

The consequences included large-scale loss of life, separation of families, displacement, hunger, disease, forced conversion, and the destruction or appropriation of homes and land. Churches, monasteries, schools, cemeteries, libraries, and manuscript collections were damaged or lost. Survivors established new communities in Iraq, Syria, Lebanon, Iran, Armenia, Europe, the Americas, and Australia, while others remained in or returned to parts of their historic homeland.


Precise casualty figures remain difficult to establish. Records were destroyed, populations were displaced across borders, and different studies use different geographic boundaries and definitions of who should be counted. Some Aramean accounts cite an estimate that as many as 85% of the Aramean population in the most heavily affected areas was killed, deported, or otherwise lost during the genocide. This figure should not be presented as a precise empire-wide mortality rate: population baselines, geographic scope, and whether displacement is included differ. The evidence nevertheless supports describing the losses as catastrophic and involving hundreds of thousands of people [1] [2] [3].

Memory and Historical Recognition

Seyfo remains central to the collective memory of Aramean communities. Commemorations preserve the names of destroyed villages, displaced families, clergy, teachers, and cultural institutions. They also connect the experience of survivors with later efforts to maintain language, worship, manuscripts, family histories, and community identity in the diaspora.


The history of Seyfo is closely connected to the Armenian genocide, but the communities and historical records should not be merged without explanation. Comparative study can identify shared policies, military settings, and local dynamics while still preserving the distinct experiences and terminology of each population. Responsible writing should distinguish documented evidence, survivor testimony, demographic reconstruction, and later political interpretation.

Conclusion

Seyfo was a regional and interconnected process of mass violence, deportation, dispossession, and cultural destruction during the First World War. Its effects differed across provinces and communities, but the destruction of homes, institutions, and lives created a lasting rupture in the history of Aramean Christians. Studying the period requires both recognition of the scale of the catastrophe and care with names, estimates, local responsibility, and the limits of surviving evidence.
