The Chaldeans (Kaldu) were West Semitic Aramean tribes who settled in southern Babylonia and ultimately conquered and destroyed the Assyrian Empire.

The Chaldeans, known in Assyrian records as Kaldu (or Kashdu), were among the Aramean tribal groups that migrated into southern Mesopotamia during the late second and early first millennium BC [1]. They settled in the marshlands and along the rivers of southern Babylonia, an area that came to be known as Chaldea. By the 9th century BC, the entire region from Babylon to the Mediterranean coast was in the hands of Aramean tribes known collectively as Kaldu - the biblical Chaldeans [2].


The Hebrew name "Kasdim" and the Assyro-Babylonian "Kaldu" refer to the same people. The name may derive from Kesed, nephew of Abraham [6], or from the Akkadian root "kasadu" meaning "to capture" - suggesting their origins as semi-nomadic tribal raiders [3].

Rise to Power

The Kaldu tribes were attested in Assyrian texts from the early 9th century BC [4]. Despite repeated Assyrian campaigns to subdue them, the Chaldean tribes maintained their resistance. Several Chaldean leaders seized the throne of Babylon at various points, most notably Merodach-Baladan II, who twice ruled as king of Babylon in defiance of Assyrian authority.


In 626 BC, the Chaldean general Nabopolassar proclaimed himself king of Babylon, establishing the Neo-Babylonian (Chaldean) dynasty and launching a war of independence against Assyrian rule [5].

The Fall of Assyria

According to the Babylonian Chronicle tablets discovered in the 19th century, the Assyrians and Babylonians engaged in a prolonged 12-year struggle, with Assyria also facing internal conflicts.


In the tenth year of Nabopolassar (616 BC), the Babylonians defeated the Assyrian army and marched up the river, sacking Mane, Sahiri, and Balihu. One year after the victory, the Assyrians mustered their army, recaptured their losses, and succeeded in driving the Babylonians back to Takritain. Nabopolassar stationed his army in the fortress of Takritain, where the two armies continued their battle until the Assyrians were beaten again and retreated.


Around 614 BC, Nabopolassar formed a powerful alliance with the Medes under Cyaxares, the Scythians, and other tribes to overthrow the Assyrian Empire once and for all.


In 612 BC, the allied forces besieged and sacked Nineveh, the Assyrian capital. After a brutal battle, the city fell, leading to the complete destruction of the Neo-Assyrian Empire within a few years.

The Neo-Babylonian Empire

Following the fall of Assyria, the Chaldean dynasty established the Neo-Babylonian Empire, which became the most powerful state of its time. Under Nebuchadnezzar II (c. 605-561 BC), the empire extended from the Taurus Mountains in Anatolia to eastern Arabia and deep into southern Iran [5].


In the Neo-Babylonian Empire, Chaldeans, Arameans, and Babylonians became largely indistinguishable [1]. The Aramaic language, already the lingua franca of the region, served as the administrative language of the empire alongside Akkadian.


The Neo-Babylonian Empire lasted until 539 BC, when it was conquered by the Achaemenid Persians under Cyrus the Great - less than a century after the founding of the Chaldean dynasty.
